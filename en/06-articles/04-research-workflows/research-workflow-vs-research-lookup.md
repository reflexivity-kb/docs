# Research Workflow vs. Research Lookup

Finding a document, number, or headline is an important research step, but it is not the same as completing the research. Real investment work usually continues into interpretation, comparison, relationship analysis, and monitoring.

The distinction is useful because many AI tools are excellent at lookup while the broader workflow still has to be assembled around the answer.

## Lookup answers a question; workflow carries the research forward

A terminal, search system, or document database can quickly return a price, filing, transcript, or published piece of research.

That is useful and often necessary.

But the analyst still has to decide what else is relevant, connect the information across sources, and carry the work into the next step.

## A workflow connects multiple stages

Reflexivity is designed to support research across several stages.

The Knowledge Graph helps identify relationships and exposures. Alfred can run deeper analysis and historical comparisons. Monitoring can continue after the original question is answered. Supporting evidence remains traceable to source.

The point is not to eliminate lookup. It is to connect lookup with the analytical work that comes before and after it.

## Workflows reduce repeated setup

When research remains a sequence of isolated lookups, the analyst repeatedly rebuilds the context.

A persistent workflow can keep the question, relevant universe, and prior analysis available for later use.

That makes research more repeatable and less dependent on one person's memory of the previous steps.

## Evaluate the whole process

The distinction matters when evaluating AI research tools.

A system that retrieves information well may still leave most of the research workflow to the user.

The more useful question is whether the tool supports the path from identifying what matters to analyzing it, checking the evidence, and continuing the work over time.

## Retrieval is an input, not the end state

A lookup answers “what does this source say?” or “what is this value?”

Research usually asks “what does this mean, what else is connected, how does it compare with history, and what should I watch next?”

Those questions require orchestration across multiple inputs and stages. They may involve a document, a time series, a peer set, a thematic exposure, and a scenario in the same analysis.

That is why a workflow layer matters even when every individual source is already searchable. The bottleneck is often not finding one piece of information; it is carrying the evidence through the full chain of reasoning without losing context.
