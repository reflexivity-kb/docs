Collecting facts about a company is different from understanding what that company is connected to. For many investment questions, the second problem is more important because market effects travel through products, themes, geographies, suppliers, customers, and peers.

Relationship intelligence makes those connections part of the research context instead of leaving them to be rediscovered manually each time.

## The research question is often “what else is connected?”

A research question often begins with one company or event.

The relevant market impact may extend to competitors, customers, suppliers, products, themes, or regions that are not named directly.

Relationship intelligence helps identify those connections so the research universe is not limited to the entity that appeared in the original query.

Illustrative August 2026 relationship view: starting from one company, the graph connects companies, themes, geographies, products, and a macro driver to show possible second-order paths.

## A context layer for fragmented information

Reflexivity's Knowledge Graph maps relationships across companies, products, themes, markets, and geographies.

Alfred can use those relationships to reason across a wider investment context and identify possible second-order effects.

## Relationship intelligence supports both queries and monitoring

When the investor asks a question, the graph can help define which entities and exposures deserve analysis.

When Alfred detects a market event, the same relationship context can help identify where the implications may propagate.

## The graph is evidence-backed

Relationships in the Knowledge Graph are designed to carry source evidence so the user can inspect why a connection exists.

For deeper methodology, exposure scoring, and evidence handling, see the access-controlled [Reflexivity Knowledge Graph](https://github.com/reflexivity-kb/knowledge-graph) documentation.

## Relationship intelligence answers a different question from retrieval

Retrieval asks: “What information can I find about this company?”

Relationship intelligence asks: “What else is connected to this company, why is it connected, and how could an external change propagate through those connections?”

That distinction is what makes the graph useful for second-order research.

A relationship can connect a company to a product, a theme, a geography, or another company. The relationship itself carries evidence, so the user can inspect why the connection exists rather than accepting an unexplained peer or exposure list.

This context can then be used in both directions: start with a company and map outward, or start with a theme or event and identify which companies may be exposed.
