# How Financial Institutions Across Major Markets Are Adopting AI: What Official Evidence Shows

Financial institutions are not converging on one global AI rulebook. They operate under different legal systems, supervisory models, market structures, and technology environments.

But the official evidence is beginning to converge on a common operating problem.

As AI moves from experimentation into production, institutions are asking increasingly similar questions about **data quality and provenance, workflow control, human oversight, third-party dependencies, explainability, monitoring, and accountability**.

This article compares official material from Japan, the United States, the United Kingdom, the European Union, South Korea, China, Hong Kong, and Singapore. Across these markets, three operating questions recur: **Can institutions trust the data and evidence path? Can AI enter the research workflow at the right point in the decision process? Can people review, explain, and govern the result?**

## The pattern at a glance

| Market | Official evidence | Main signal |
|---|---|---|
| Japan | Bank of Japan, FSA | GenAI adoption is broad; governance, third-party risk, safety, and data readiness remain active constraints |
| United States | U.S. Treasury, FINRA | Data lineage, vendor transparency, accuracy, and third-party risk are becoming core operating questions |
| United Kingdom | Bank of England, FCA | AI adoption is already mainstream; four of the five highest-rated current risks are data-related |
| European Union | EBA, ECB Banking Supervision | AI is moving beyond pilots while data governance, transparency, and third-party dependencies remain central |
| South Korea | Financial Services Commission | Financial institutions have identified infrastructure, domain data, and governance gaps as practical adoption barriers |
| China | National Financial Regulatory Administration | Data quality, explainability, monitoring, audit trails, and human review are explicit operating requirements |
| Hong Kong | HKMA | Responsible adoption is tied to transparency, validation, explainability, governance, and manual intervention |
| Singapore | MAS | AI governance is being framed as a lifecycle discipline covering inventory, data, human oversight, third parties, testing, and monitoring |

The regulatory details differ. The operating questions do not.

## Japan: adoption is moving into core operations

The Bank of Japan's FY2026 survey covered 150 financial institutions and found that **more than 90% were using or trialing generative AI**. Use was expanding from general administrative work into core operations that use customer information.

The same survey also found that progress in adoption did not remove the need for control. Governance, third-party risk management, safety and security, IT infrastructure, and data readiness remained areas where many institutions saw room for improvement or further work.

Japan's Financial Services Agency has also continued to develop its AI Discussion Paper through public-private dialogue on AI use, governance, risk management, and regulatory interpretation.

The Japanese signal is therefore not simply "AI adoption is high." It is that the operating burden changes as AI gets closer to consequential work.

## United States: institutions need to understand the data supply chain

The U.S. Treasury has highlighted risks involving data privacy, bias, and third-party providers as AI use expands across financial services.

Its work on AI-specific cybersecurity risk goes further. Treasury recommended stronger **data supply-chain mapping** and discussed a standardized "nutrition label" concept for vendor-provided AI systems and data providers. The idea is straightforward: financial institutions should be able to understand what data was used to train a model, where that data came from, and how data submitted to the model will be used.

That is a provenance problem, not merely a model-performance problem.

FINRA's 2026 Regulatory Oversight material points in the same direction from a securities-industry perspective. Firms have been implementing GenAI primarily for internal processes and information retrieval, with summarization and information extraction the most common observed use case. FINRA also highlights risks involving hallucination, inaccurate or outdated data, bias, cybersecurity, and third-party vendors.

For institutional research, the implication is clear: retrieving and summarizing information is useful only when the evidence path remains trustworthy.

## United Kingdom: adoption is high, and the highest-rated risks are mostly about data

The Bank of England and FCA's 2024 survey found that **75% of respondent firms were already using AI**, with another 10% planning to do so within three years.

A third of current AI use cases were third-party implementations. The survey also found that **four of the five highest-rated current AI risks were data-related**:

- data privacy and protection;
- data quality;
- data security;
- data bias and representativeness.

Data governance was used as an AI governance component by 79% of firms already using AI.

The survey is useful because it shows adoption and control moving together. Firms are not waiting for AI to disappear. They are building governance around a technology they increasingly expect to use.

## European Union: moving beyond pilots increases the importance of control

The European Banking Authority describes AI adoption as broad across EU and EEA banks. Its more recent work indicates that, compared with 2022, only a small number of banks remained in pilot testing by 2024, with many initiatives moving further into banks' IT infrastructure.

At the same time, EBA highlights transparency, ICT risk, data governance, data quality, reliability, privacy, and dependence on third-party providers. Many banks use third-party services, including cloud APIs from large model developers.

ECB Banking Supervision makes the broader data point even more directly: robust risk-data aggregation and reporting capabilities are a prerequisite for sound risk management, and deficiencies in data quality and reporting can undermine both risk management and the use of advanced technologies such as AI.

In other words, AI does not bypass the institution's existing data discipline. It makes weaknesses in that discipline more consequential.

## South Korea: model access is not the same as financial-domain readiness

South Korea provides another useful angle.

In December 2024, the Financial Services Commission said domestic financial institutions had raised three practical obstacles to wider GenAI use:

- insufficient AI infrastructure;
- insufficient data;
- lack of clear governance for generative AI use.

The policy response included support for AI infrastructure, finance-specific data, and revision of financial-sector AI guidelines.

This matters because it separates access to a capable general-purpose model from the ability to use AI in specialized financial work. Financial-domain vocabulary, rules, data structures, entitlements, and operational constraints still have to be supported.

The coverage question therefore becomes concrete: **does the system have the right evidence for this financial task, not merely the ability to understand the prompt?**

## China: explainability and human review are becoming explicit operating controls

China's National Financial Regulatory Administration issued 2026 guidance on safe AI development and application in banking and insurance.

The guidance requires institutions to pay attention to the quality, quantity, and distribution of training data, establish transparency and explainability standards for high-risk scenarios, and preserve records around model development and change.

It is especially direct on human responsibility. Where AI lacks sufficient explainability in high-risk contexts, it should serve only as an auxiliary tool and the final decision should be made by a person. For important decisions affecting customer rights or producing material financial impact, institutions are expected to establish human review points and retain original data, reasoning paths, and threshold-trigger records so accountability can be traced.

Earlier banking and insurance data-security rules similarly require AI and model management to be verifiable, auditable, and traceable, with pre-use data-security reviews and ongoing monitoring of automated processing.

This is a strong example of Data and Compliance becoming part of the operating architecture rather than an after-the-fact disclosure.

## Hong Kong: responsible adoption is tied to validation and explainability

The Hong Kong Monetary Authority's 2024 research examined 59 GenAI use cases, including 51 that were specific to financial services.

The paper places GenAI adoption inside broader responsible-AI principles such as transparency and disclosure. HKMA's supervisory principles on big-data analytics and AI also emphasize governance and accountability, explainability, validation before launch, ongoing review, reliability and accuracy, and the ability to intervene manually where appropriate.

The important point is not that every Hong Kong AI use case is regulated in exactly the same way. It is that the supervisory model expects financial institutions to remain able to understand, validate, and own the system's behavior.

## Singapore: governance is being designed as a full lifecycle

In 2025, the Monetary Authority of Singapore proposed AI Risk Management Guidelines building in part on a 2024 thematic review of key banks' AI use.

The proposed framework covers:

- board and senior-management oversight;
- accurate and current AI inventories;
- risk materiality assessment;
- data management;
- transparency and explainability;
- human oversight;
- third-party risk;
- evaluation and testing;
- monitoring and change management.

MAS's Project MindForge similarly developed a financial-industry GenAI risk framework and enterprise reference architecture.

Singapore's framing is useful because it treats responsible AI as a lifecycle: identify the system, assess materiality, control the data and model, test it, monitor it, and keep accountable humans in the process.

## What repeats across markets

These jurisdictions do not share one identical regulatory model, and the cited materials are not all the same type. They include surveys, supervisory observations, discussion papers, guidance, and formal rules.

They should not be read as interchangeable legal requirements.

But they repeatedly surface three operating layers.

### 1. Data: Can the institution trust the evidence path?

The recurring questions include:

- Where did the data come from?
- Is it current, complete, and appropriate for the task?
- How is third-party data or model dependency handled?
- What happens when data is missing, stale, conflicting, or outside coverage?
- Can the institution trace what evidence influenced the result?

These are the questions developed in [Data Trust Is Becoming the Adoption Layer for AI in Institutional Research](data-trust-is-becoming-the-adoption-layer-for-ai-in-institutional-research.md).

### 2. Flow: Can AI operate inside the real workflow?

The official materials also point beyond static model quality.

Institutions need inventories, monitoring, testing, human checkpoints, change management, lifecycle controls, and integration into existing operating processes.

That is a workflow question: can AI arrive at the right point in the process with the right information and the right controls around it?

### 3. Compliance: Can the institution explain and own the outcome?

Governance, accountability, explainability, auditability, third-party risk, human oversight, and records of model/data use recur across markets.

The practical standard is not simply whether an AI system can produce an answer. It is whether the institution can explain how the answer was produced, review it when needed, and remain accountable for the decision.

## Why this matters for institutional research

Investment research sits at the intersection of all three layers.

An analyst may need to know:

1. **Source**: where did the claim or number come from?
2. **Time**: what data timestamp and market clock does it represent?
3. **Coverage**: is there sufficient evidence for this asset, market, and question?
4. **Workflow**: did the analysis run at the right point in the research process with the necessary data available?
5. **Accountability**: can a human review the evidence, understand important limitations, and defend the resulting decision?

The markets reviewed here use different language, but the direction is remarkably consistent: **production-grade AI in finance is becoming an operating-model problem, not just a model-capability problem.**

## Three operating questions that recur across markets

The official evidence reviewed here points to three connected operating questions.

1. **Data:** Can the institution verify where a number or claim came from, what time it represents, and whether the coverage is sufficient for the question?
2. **Workflow:** Does the analysis arrive at the point in the research and decision process where it can actually be used?
3. **Review and accountability:** Can people inspect the evidence, understand material limitations, intervene when needed, and explain the final judgment?

These are not isolated checkboxes. As AI moves into production, they become parts of the same operating model.
